Buying tickets: asking for two

Japanese dialogue with English explanations, substitutions, repeat gaps and an active response.

The bears have reached the station and are standing at a ticket machine. Before thinking about complicated ticket types, let us listen for two simple things: what they want to buy, and how many. Picture two friends traveling together, with each person needing a ticket. What number does the friend confirm?

切符を二枚買おう。／うん、二枚だね。

The scarf bear suggests buying two tickets. Her friend confirms the number: yes, two. That repetition has a practical purpose. It helps both travelers check that they are buying the right amount. We will understand their shared decision first, then practice telling a member of staff the quantity we need.

切符を二枚買おう。

The first line means, let’s buy two tickets. It is a suggestion to a friend about what to do together, rather than a direct request to a ticket clerk. Listen for the item and then the quantity. You can follow the practical meaning before understanding every individual sound.

うん、二枚だね。

The reply confirms two tickets. It shows that the friends agree about the number. It does not add a destination or tell us whether these are return tickets. Do not fill in missing details just because you know a typical travel routine. Start with the information the conversation really provides.

Our main point is counting tickets. Japanese uses a counter for thin, flat things such as tickets and sheets of paper. Listen to a request you could make to a member of staff. Notice the quantity inside it. This request has a different ending from the friends’ suggestion to buy together.

切符を二枚ください。

That means, two tickets, please. The item and quantity stay the same, but the ending now asks the listener to provide them. We have changed the social situation, so we have changed what the sentence does. A line that works between friends does not automatically fit every conversation at a counter.

Practice the number and counter together. Today, we only need one, two, and three tickets. This counter is not a general word for counting every object; cups and animals use other expressions. Keeping the practice with tickets helps us learn one useful connection without opening several new grammar topics.

切符を一枚ください。

切符を三枚ください。

Now imagine changing the number of travelers. One person needs one ticket; three people need three. The request stays familiar while the quantity changes. You could place one, two, and three pieces of paper on a table as you listen, so the sound connects to something you can actually count.

Now listen once, then repeat during the gap. Keep the meaning in mind instead of chasing every change in pitch. There is time to finish each sentence. Start quietly if you like, then try again with a clear, comfortable voice.

切符を二枚買おう。

うん、二枚だね。

切符を一枚ください。

Your turn. You and one friend are at the ticket window, and each of you needs a ticket. Ask the member of staff for the right number. First count the people, then name the item and quantity, and finish the request. This time, your friend is not there to check the number for you.

切符を二枚ください。

The model answer asks for two tickets. The question before it asked how many tickets you needed. If you thought of two and put it into a clear request, you have completed the task. Now listen to the original again, where the friends suggest buying together and confirm their shared plan.
